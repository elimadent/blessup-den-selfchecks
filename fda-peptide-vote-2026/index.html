Advisory Committee Record · 23–24 July 2026

Six peptides. One vote. Nothing is legal yet.

An FDA advisory committee recommended six of seven peptides for the compounding list, over the unanimous objection of the agency's own scientists. Here is what that actually does to access, to price, and to the grey market most of us are buying from.

The vote record

What the committee actually decided

On 23 and 24 July 2026, the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee reviewed seven peptides for the 503A Bulk Drug Substances List, the roster of raw ingredients a licensed compounding pharmacy may legally use when making a prescription for one named patient.

Each peptide was voted twice, once as free base and once as the acetate salt, for fourteen voting questions in total. Every peptide broke identically across both forms.

Why the totals keep changing

The number of votes cast differs from question to question, which looks like sloppy reporting and mostly is not. Most of the temporary voting members FDA added were restricted to the peptides matching their specialty: a hepatologist voted only on BPC-157, a dermatologist only on KPV and TB-500, two neurologists only on Semax, a pain and addiction specialist only on emideltide. So the eligible pool genuinely changed each time.

Corrected 25 July. An earlier version of this page showed Epitalon at 7–5–1 and Semax at 8–5–0, following the looser summaries in general coverage. The detailed trade reporting gives Epitalon 7 yes, 4 no, 1 abstention and Semax 8 yes, 5 no, 1 abstention, and those are the figures shown above. The Epitalon total of 12 is consistent with one member being excluded from that topic. The direction of every vote was never in dispute.

The indications

These were medical votes, not wellness votes

This is the detail the viral version leaves out, and it is the one most likely to matter to you later. The committee did not vote on "gut health" or "longevity." Each peptide was reviewed against a specific medical indication, and a prescriber will eventually have to write one of these on a real prescription.

A committee weighing BPC-157 for an inflammatory bowel disease is not a committee blessing it for general recovery. Those are different questions with different evidence bars, and the gap between the two columns above is where a lot of disappointment is going to live.

Status check

What changed on the day of the vote: nothing

An advisory committee recommends. FDA decides. To actually add a substance, the agency has to review the record, publish a proposed rule, open a comment period, weigh the comments, and publish a final rule. Reporting puts that at up to a year, and rulemaking routinely runs longer.

FDA is free to decline. Given that its own reviewers said no to all seven, declining is a live possibility rather than a technicality.

The practical test

Nobody can fill a BPC-157 prescription because of this vote. No product became legal. No vendor became legitimate. If a seller cites this vote as proof that what they ship is now approved, that is a lie, and it tells you what you need to know about the seller.

The part almost everyone has wrong

A lot of people in this space believe the legal question was already settled in the spring. It was not, and the real sequence matters.

Late 2023
Nineteen peptides moved to Category 2

FDA places 19 peptides in its "may present significant safety risks" bucket, effectively ending legal compounding. BPC-157 is added in September 2023.

27 February 2026
The Rogan announcement

The HHS Secretary says on the Joe Rogan podcast that roughly 14 of the 19 are expected to return to Category 1, with an announcement coming "within a couple of weeks." This is where most of the "peptides are legal again" confidence comes from.

Never
That announcement was not published

FDA did not publish the promised Category 1 move. The statement was intent, not action.

April 2026
Removed from Category 2, but not promoted

FDA removes peptides including BPC-157 and TB-500 from Category 2, announced 15 April and effective 22 April. Not because it reconsidered the science, but because the original nominations were withdrawn. They were not moved to Category 1. They are not approved and have no USP monograph.

23–24 July 2026
The advisory committee votes

Six of seven recommended, against the unanimous advice of FDA's own reviewers. Non-binding.

Next
Proposed rule, comment period, final rule

The proposed rule is the document that actually changes anything, and the comment period is the point at which public input carries weight. Watch for that, not for the vote.

So the current position is a gap: not expressly prohibited, not authorized. If you have been operating since spring on the belief that reclassification already happened, that belief rests on a promise that was never executed.

FDA's objection

The agency's own scientists said no to all seven

Not some. All. FDA's briefing documents concluded that none of the seven met the four-factor framework the agency uses, citing missing human safety data, no universally agreed chemical specification, batch-to-batch inconsistency, and for injectables a risk of immune reactions driven by aggregation and peptide-related impurities.

The most quoted moment of the two days came from Russell Wesdyk, the Associate Director for Regulatory Affairs in CDER's Office of Product Quality Assessment, who put the identity problem in terms anyone can follow.

“When you write a script for TB-500, what form do you think your patients are getting? Does anybody know what form do you think your patients actually get? Does anybody know? I certainly don't, and that's a challenge for us.”
Russell Wesdyk · Associate Director for Regulatory Affairs, Office of Product Quality Assessment, FDA CDER
“We can't create quality standards until we actually know what it is.”
Russell Wesdyk · FDA CDER, on the absence of an agreed chemical specification

That is not a bureaucratic complaint. It is the observation that two vials labelled TB-500 may not contain the same molecule, which is precisely the problem a compounding pharmacy cannot solve on its own.

Access mechanics

What a listing would actually buy you

Say FDA eventually lists them. That is much narrower than "peptides are legal now."

Listing lets a state-licensed pharmacy use the bulk ingredient to make a preparation against a valid prescription written for a specific, named patient. It does not authorize retail sale of vials. It does not make the peptide an approved drug. It does not let anyone sell you a vial without a prescriber. And it does not retroactively legalize any existing grey vendor's business model.

There is also a supply problem nobody is discussing. A 503A pharmacy must source bulk ingredient from an FDA-registered facility with proper documentation. Most of what circulates today is research-grade material from Chinese suppliers, a chain with no quality infrastructure and no ability to recall a contaminated batch. A listing on paper does not put product on a shelf.

If you are drug tested, read this twice

A legal prescription does not make a substance permitted in sport. On the 2026 WADA Prohibited List, BPC-157 falls under S0 as a non-approved substance, and TB-500 is named explicitly under S2.3 as a thymosin beta-4 derivative. Both are prohibited at all times, in and out of competition.

The wrinkle that matters: S0 substances are classed as Specified, while S2 substances are non-Specified, and a non-Specified finding carries the harsher default sanction. In a BPC-157 and TB-500 stack, the TB-500 side is the one more likely to end a career. "My doctor prescribed it" is not a defense, and a 503A listing would not change that by a single word.

The two cycle figures are one clinic's advertised package pricing, on a ladder that runs $445 for a single peptide, $795 for two, $1,095 for three and $1,295 for four, with financing offered over as long as 26 months. Treat them as one seller's list price, not a market rate. They come from a single commercial page that was surfaced in research rather than independently opened, which is a weaker standard than everything else on this page.

The gap is three to seven times, and legalization probably does not close it, because the markup is not in the molecule.

Pharmaceutical-grade ingredient runs roughly three to five times research-grade, and that multiplier is driven by documentation, testing and quality systems rather than purity itself. Three to five times on an ingredient is not what turns sixty dollars a month into six hundred. The prescriber visit, the membership, the clinic wrapper and the recurring-revenue model do that.

Compounded NAD+ is the closest comparison available, a non-approved sterile injectable sold cash-pay through longevity clinics. The vial runs fifty to two hundred dollars while monthly programs run one hundred fifty to four hundred, at doses a thousand times larger than a peptide dose. The milligrams are not what you are paying for.

There is real evidence on the cheaper side too. Compounded semaglutide undercut the brand by five to seven times while it was legal. Compounded low-dose naltrexone runs twenty to sixty dollars a month and compounded HCG fifty-three to eighty-three per 10,000 IU. A sanctioned channel does not inherently carry a premium.

Honest read

The vial gets cheaper, more consistent and more accountable. Your monthly bill probably does not fall, because you will be buying a program rather than a vial. And essentially none of it will be covered by insurance, since compounded non-approved preparations are almost never covered and there is no approved alternative to have failed first. HSA and FSA funds generally can be used with a legitimate prescription, which is the one real financial break available.

The grey market

What this does to the people selling, and to you buying

Start with the thing that gets repeated most and is most wrong. "Research use only" has never been a legal shield. Under federal law a product is a drug based on its intended use, and FDA establishes intended use from website copy, social posts and surrounding circumstances, not from the disclaimer. FDA's warning letters to peptide sellers say it plainly: the evidence from the website establishes the products are intended as drugs for human use. Selling bacteriostatic water alongside the vials is treated as further evidence of human-use intent.

That enforcement is neither theoretical nor new.

Warning letters
50+

Issued across the industry by September 2025, with a documented run of letters to peptide vendors from December 2024 through June 2026.

Largest US vendor
Closed

Peptide Sciences stopped taking orders on 6 March 2026 after more than a decade, months before this vote. No public indictment was identified. It simply stopped.

Criminal exposure
Sellers

None of these six are controlled substances, so there is no DEA scheduling hook and buyers face essentially no personal criminal risk. Enforcement targets the seller.

Read that timeline again, because it undercuts both confident narratives. The grey market was already consolidating hard under enforcement pressure with no legal alternative in sight. This vote did not start that.

Two forces, pulling opposite ways

The case that it shrinks. The single best argument grey vendors and their customers have used is "there is no legal way to get this." For these six molecules specifically, that argument dies. A US-based vendor with a brand, a website, a payment processor and identifiable owners is now competing against a licensed channel while remaining an easy target, and we have already watched that exact vendor archetype break.

The case that it grows. This was the largest free advertising event these compounds have ever had, reaching millions who had never heard of BPC-157. The price gap survives legalization. Buyers face no criminal exposure. FDA's headcount did not change. And critically, these six have no brand-name manufacturer with a patent to defend, so the wave of private lawsuits strangling grey GLP-1 sellers has no equivalent here. Nobody is going to sue a BPC-157 vendor for infringing a product that does not exist.

The experiment already ran, in reverse

When the legal compounded semaglutide channel closed in April and May 2025, demand did not disappear. It migrated to research-chemical vendors. That tells us legal availability and grey volume are inversely coupled, which supports the shrink case. It also tells us price, not legality, decides where people go, which supports the growth case.

My read is bifurcation rather than a clean win or loss. The visible, branded, US-facing vendor loses. The offshore and anonymous seller does fine. The total number of people injecting these compounds goes up.

If you sell in this space, the risk is not that demand vanishes. It is that being findable becomes the liability.

And spare a thought for the one that lost

Emideltide, which is DSIP, is now in a worse position than if it had never been reviewed. Before 24 July a seller could point to regulatory silence. Now there is a documented FDA review and an advisory committee that declined to recommend it, in the same session where six siblings got through. The same logic extends to every peptide never nominated: a short blessed list creates an implied unblessed list, and everything outside it reads as more clearly illicit than it did when nothing was blessed.

Quality

The problem the vote does not fix

If you take one practical thing from this page, take this.

The grey market's entire trust apparatus is the certificate of analysis, and the COA measures the one thing that is usually fine. In independent aggregate testing of BPC-157 across hundreds of samples from dozens of vendors, HPLC purity came back consistently high. Fill quantity diverged from the advertised amount by as much as seventy-one percent. You can hold a genuine 99 percent purity COA for a vial containing a fraction, or a large excess, of what the label says.

The sterility test is almost never run, and the reason is structural rather than lazy: nobody can hold inventory for fourteen days and still run that business.

For a sense of what this means in practice, a peer-reviewed 2024 analysis of semaglutide bought from online sellers without a prescription found measured purity between 7.7 and 14.37 percent against a 99 percent label claim, and detected endotoxin in every single sample. Different compound, same supply chain, same incentives.

Why the harm record looks thin

Harm from grey vials is structurally invisible. Adverse event systems capture drugs, not research chemicals. There is no lot number to report against, no recall pathway, and people who believe they are doing something quasi-illegal do not tell their doctor what they injected. The thin harm record is not evidence of safety. It is evidence of a missing measurement system.

That is the honest case for a legal channel independent of price: a named prescriber who owns the dose, a pharmacist who checks your other medications, a lot number, a beyond-use date, a recall pathway, and somewhere to go when something goes wrong.

The split tracks professional background closely. Most reported "yes" votes came from clinicians who work with peptides directly. Most reported "no" votes came from academic and hospital specialists who do not.

That pattern is real, and it has two honest readings. You can call it a conflict, which is what several outlets did. You can equally call it expertise: the people who prescribe these compounds every week have watched patients take them, and the people voting no mostly had not. A committee of pure academics would have its own blind spot. Which reading you find persuasive is a judgment call, and this page is not going to make it for you.

What the record does not show is anyone voting yes because they were paid to. No source establishes that, FDA's ethics screening issued no conflict-of-interest waivers under the relevant federal statute, and correlation between someone's job and their vote is not proof of motive. Treat the tables as information about who was in the room, not as an accusation.

It also matters that the yes side made a substantive argument, not a commercial one. Their case was that the legal standard for adding a bulk substance under section 503A is not the standard for approving a new drug, and that refusing access pushes people toward an unregulated supply that is measurably worse. Read the next section before deciding what you think, because the argument in the room was better than the argument online.

Two caveats on the tables themselves: individual votes were only reported for some members, and David Pope voted yes on BPC-157 with no reported peptide-selling practice, so the pattern is strong rather than perfect. No vote was inferred from anyone's employer. The one member who abstained throughout, Timothy Fensky, represents the National Association of Boards of Pharmacy, which took no position.

The argument

In their own words

The transcript is more interesting than any summary of it, because both sides made a real argument. Here is the actual exchange.

The case for yes: harm reduction

The yes side did not argue the evidence was good. They argued that people are already injecting this, and that a regulated version is safer than what is being bought now.

“Just last week, I had a patient whose research-grade whatever that they got from some place that they brought to me to fix was laced with MDMA and ecstasy. That's not protecting the American people.”
Melissa Loseke, DO · standing member, wellness clinic owner · explaining a conditional yes
Our take

We checked this quote and she really did say it. We are not calling anyone a liar. But we are not passing this along as fact either, and we want to say why plainly.

Nothing about that story matches what actually turns up when grey vials get tested. The documented problems are manufacturing problems: fill quantity off by as much as 71 percent from the label, endotoxin in every sample in one peer-reviewed analysis, purity misses, and sometimes the wrong peptide altogether. That is sloppy production, and it is bad enough on its own.

A peptide vial containing MDMA is a different kind of claim entirely. It would mean either deliberate adulteration or cross-contamination from a completely unrelated production line, and neither shows up anywhere in the published testing data. Worth noting too that MDMA and ecstasy are the same substance, so "laced with MDMA and ecstasy" is saying the same thing twice. No lab report was entered into the record.

So we are filing this one as a story told to win a vote, not as evidence. The real testing data makes the case better than the scary version does, and we would rather hand you something that holds up when somebody pushes back on it. If we ever put a claim in front of you that we cannot stand behind, call it out. That is how this is supposed to work.

The single best artifact

The researcher who discovered BPC-157 is listed as chief executive of a company whose own website carries a page titled "BPC 157 Stable Patent rights for sale." The molecule is patented, and the patent is openly advertised for sale. It still produced no approved drug.

So what is the real reason nobody developed them?

Here is the honest version of the argument the viral post is groping toward, because there is a real economic story. It is just not suppression.

A US patent runs twenty years from filing. Patents filed on these compounds in the late 1980s and 1990s have already expired or are close to it. An expired patent means anyone can make the molecule, which means no company can recoup the roughly one to two billion dollars and ten-plus years that FDA approval demands. The incentive is gone, not because a conspiracy removed it, but because the clock ran out.

There is a second reason, and the peer-reviewed literature names it directly. A 2026 review in Pharmaceutics concluded that the primary barrier to clinical translation is "the absence of fundamental pharmaceutical science: characterized formulations, validated pharmacokinetics, and a coherent drug development strategy." Not suppression. Missing homework.

And one detail that quietly refutes the whole conspiracy frame: a biotech did license the MOTS-c intellectual property and take an analog through early human trials. Somebody tried. The category is not forbidden. It is just hard, expensive, and unrewarding once the patent clock has run.

"Unpatentable" is a story that explains away missing evidence. The missing evidence is the actual problem.

Where this leaves you

Three honest conclusions

If you use these compounds: nothing changed this week. Do not let a headline talk you into treating a research vial as a blessed product, and do not let a seller cite this vote as cover. If a legal route opens, expect it to cost more per month and be worth considering anyway, because you are buying oversight and accountability rather than milligrams. If you are drug tested in any capacity, that calculation is stricter for you.

If you sell in this space: the exposure is on you, not your customers, and "research use only" is not the protection you have been told it is. The enforcement trend was already steep before this vote, and a blessed list makes everything off that list look worse.

For everyone: watch for the proposed rule. That document, not this vote, is the thing that actually changes your life, and the comment period is where public input carries weight.
